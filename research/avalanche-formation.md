# Dry-slab avalanche formation: what the profile data say (read 2026-09-15)

Compact notes from Schweizer & Jamieson (2003), *Snowpack properties for snow profile analysis*, CRST 37, 233–241 (PDF read directly), supplemented by the shear-frame papers in slab-mechanics-for-simulation.md.

- A dry slab avalanche needs a cohesive slab over a thin weak layer or interface, on a bed surface. In 424 skier-triggered and skied-but-stable profiles from Switzerland and Canada, the failure layer had median thickness 3 cm; slabs were 45–47 cm thick with median density 130–200 kg/m³.
- What separated unstable from stable profiles (p < 0.001, both countries): low rutschblock score (< 4), large failure-layer grains (≥ 1.25 mm), soft failure layer (≤ fist plus), and large differences in grain size (≥ 0.75 mm) and hardness (≥ 1.7 steps) across the failure interface. Elevation, aspect and slope angle did not differ between the groups.
- Persistent grain types (surface hoar, facets, depth hoar) dominated failure layers; in Canada surface hoar dominated both groups, so grain type alone did not discriminate there.
- "Softer slabs increase the skier stress and strain at the depth of the failure layer. Large and persistent grains produce particularly weak failure layers due to poor bonding, and as hardness is related to bonding as well, low hardness of the failure layer also favours instability. A large difference in grain size across the failure layer indicates poor bonding between the layers. A large difference in hardness promotes stress/strain concentrations at the layer boundary." (p. 239–240)
- Failure-layer shear strength: unstable median 0.77 kPa, stable 1.48 kPa; load above the weak layer 0.7–0.9 kPa.
- Classification trees using grain-size difference, hardness difference, failure-layer hardness and slab hardness classified 65–78 % of profiles correctly.
